an open letter to the district of columbia

I walked out of a CVS with my coconut water and post-it notes to what should have been an unremarkably dull Wednesday morning but instead I watched a man try to steal my bike. I probably should have just let it go -- but you know me. Unless I saw a blade switch or another handheld weaponry of some sort, I was under the impression that I could take on this emaciated and squalid man. The police, upon arrival, blamed me for intervening mid-theft. Anyway, I am now the proud owner of a torn neck muscle and cracked ribs, souvenirs from a city that prides itself on being the beating heart of the free world!!

Congratulations, DC. You've done it again.

I want to be clear: this is completely fine. It is absolutely normal and expected that a person cannot leave a locked bike outside a pharmacy in broad daylight without it becoming someone else's property. It is perfectly reasonable that when I objected to this arrangement, I was met with violence. Because what did I expect, exactly? Accountability? Consequence?

I learned that the homeless guy's name is Trevor. He is a fentanyl addict living without shelter in one of the wealthiest, most educated, most politically mobilized cities in the United States -- home to more graduate degrees per square mile than almost anywhere on earth. We have the infrastructure to legislate the world's problems from climate change to international human rights. And yet somehow, we cannot figure out how to house, treat, or humanize the people sleeping on the grates of our metro. Honestly, I do not blame Trevor. He does not know any better. Now he's probably withdrawing from his jail cell and suffering probably more than I am at this moment. I blame the revolving door of municipal leadership that treats homelessness as an optics problem rather than a public health crisis. And I'm not even woke by any means!!

Moving back to DC this year, I'm reminded of all the performative and nonperformative work that takes place here. We march for equality of all varying sorts and vote for progress and then watch our most vulnerable neighbors disappear into drugs on the very street corners of the luxury apartments we slink into each evening. We are very good at talking about suffering. We are considerably less good at ending it.

So yes, I am in pain. Emotionally. (Right now, not physically because I'm on painkillers.) But I am not angry at a desperate man doing desperate things in a city that gave him nowhere else to go. I am furious at the 95% Democratic base in this city that is allowing for this to happen without relief.
